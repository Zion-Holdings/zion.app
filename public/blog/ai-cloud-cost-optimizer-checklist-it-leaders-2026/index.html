Cloud Cost AI Optimizer: The 12-Point Checklist Every IT Leader Needs Before Deployment

Cloud bills grow faster than teams can review them. An AI cloud cost optimizer can find waste, predict spend, and recommend right-sizing — but only if you set it up on the right foundation. This checklist covers the 12 things to verify before you connect your first billing account.

Who this is for

IT leaders, cloud architects, and FinOps practitioners managing AWS, GCP, or Azure environments with monthly spend above $10,000. If your cloud bill has grown 20% or more year-over-year without a clear driver, this checklist is for you.

The Checklist

1

Tagging Coverage

Every billable resource should have at minimum: environment (prod/staging/dev), team, and cost-center tags. Untagged resources are invisible to showback/chargeback and impossible to optimize with confidence. Aim for 95%+ tag coverage before running any optimizer.

2

Billing Data Access

The optimizer needs read-only access to your billing and usage data. Verify you can export Cost and Usage Reports (CUR) on AWS, Billing Export on GCP, or Usage Details on Azure — with at least 90 days of historical data. More history = better forecasting.

3

Baseline Spend by Service

Before any AI runs, know your starting point. Break down spend by service family (compute, storage, database, network, managed services) and by environment. This baseline is the reference point for measuring optimizer impact.

4

Idle Resource Identification

The easiest wins are resources that are running but not serving traffic. Identify unattached EIPs, idle load balancers, unmounted volumes, stopped instances still accruing storage costs, and underutilized reserved instances. These are the low-risk, high-return targets for the first optimization pass.

5

Right-Sizing Data Window

Right-sizing recommendations are only as good as the usage window they're based on. Use at least 30 days of CPU, memory, and network metrics. For workloads with seasonal patterns, extend to 90 days. Shorter windows produce dangerous over-aggressive downsizing recommendations.

6

Reserved Instance / Savings Plan Coverage

Check your current RI and Savings Plan coverage and utilization. Under-utilized commitments are money left on the table. The optimizer should factor in existing commitments before recommending new ones — otherwise you'll double-count savings.

7

Anomaly Detection Baseline

Configure anomaly detection thresholds before the optimizer goes live. Define what "normal" spend looks like per service and per day. The AI should flag spikes that deviate from the baseline, not just report aggregate trends. Quick detection = smaller bills.

8

Budget Alert Integration

Connect the optimizer's findings to your existing budget alerts. If the AI identifies a projected 30% spend increase next month, your team should get alerted before the bill arrives — not after. Align alert thresholds with your finance team's approval limits.

9

Stakeholder Map

Optimization recommendations affect real teams. Map out who owns each workload category and how recommendations will be routed. A right-sizing suggestion for a production database needs a different approval path than a dev environment cleanup. Build the routing before the first report lands.

10

Change Window Policy

Define when optimizations can be applied. Production changes on Fridays are a recipe for midnight pages. Set change windows, required approvals for critical workloads, and automatic exclusion rules for systems that cannot tolerate interruption.

11

Savings Validation Plan

Define how you will prove the optimizer worked. Track actual vs. projected savings per recommendation. Establish a monthly review cadence. If you can't measure the delta, you can't justify the investment or build trust with the team — and the next budget cycle will cut the tool.

12

Multi-Cloud Consolidation View

If you run workloads across AWS, GCP, and Azure, the optimizer should give you a single unified view — not three separate dashboards. Duplication across clouds is one of the most expensive blind spots. The first pass should identify workloads that could be consolidated to a single provider without sacrificing resilience.

What Happens Next

Once these 12 items are in place, an AI cloud cost optimizer can run continuously: scanning for waste, forecasting spend, recommending right-sizing, and tracking validated savings. The difference between a tool you check once and one that drives ongoing FinOps is the foundation you build before the first run.
